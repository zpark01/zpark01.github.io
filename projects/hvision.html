A waist-worn smartphone perception platform that combines forward LiDAR depth, downward leg video, IMU, and voice annotation for look-ahead terrain sensing and gait analysis.

System design

I used an iPhone as a reference sensing platform rather than a final production sensor. It provided LiDAR, multiple cameras, a 100 Hz IMU, audio, storage, and compute in one synchronized device.

An early design tried to use the mirror for depth sensing, but the reflected geometry produced unreliable LiDAR data. I separated the sensing roles instead. The wide camera and LiDAR remained forward-facing, while a 45 degree prism redirected only the ultra-wide camera toward the legs.

Synchronized data logging

I built a Swift and SwiftUI logger that recorded LiDAR depth at about 29 fps, downward RGB video at about 30 fps, 100 Hz device motion, camera intrinsics, and audio. All streams were timestamped using the same host clock.

Terrain perception

My first attempts estimated terrain angle from the depth map and from a fitted 3D plane. These methods were unreliable because the waist-mounted camera often saw walls and distant scene structure rather than a clean ground plane.

Inspecting the raw depth maps led to a simpler approach. Vertical depth profiles showed distinct terrain structure. Flat ground changed gradually, stair ascent formed stepped plateaus, and stair descent occupied a narrower depth range.

A simple top-versus-bottom profile feature reached an effect size of 4.11 between flat ground and stair ascent in one session.

Stair arrival prediction

For assistive control, detecting a stair is not enough. The device also needs to know when the wearer will reach it. I filtered the forward distance signal, estimated closing speed with linear regression, and computed time to contact.

On the strongest long-range trial, arrival prediction reached about 0.46 s MAE at 5 s, 0.95 s at 4 s, and 1.42 s at 3 s lead time. Performance degraded near the stair because users naturally slowed down, violating the constant-speed assumption.

Gait sensing and hands-free annotation

The downward camera was used to estimate gait rhythm with dense optical flow. I removed common frame motion caused by the torso and extracted the remaining leg movement as a one-dimensional signal.

The flat-ground session produced about 102 steps per minute with a spectral SNR of 19.6. I also replaced handheld button annotation with spoken activity labels processed offline using Whisper, allowing sessions to be labeled naturally while walking.

Limitations

H Vision demonstrated useful terrain, gait, and approach signals, but generalization was not established. Terrain features varied across sessions, and the strongest arrival prediction result relied on a limited number of long, straight stair approaches.

I therefore treated the phone as a reference platform for deciding which sensing signals were worth moving into lower-cost dedicated hardware.
