A waist-worn multimodal sensing platform I proposed and built to explore whether a consumer smartphone could provide look-ahead terrain perception, direct observation of leg motion, and hands-free annotation for walking-assist devices.

Why use a smartphone?

The goal was not to turn an iPhone into the final production sensor. I treated it as a rich reference platform: a modern phone already provides LiDAR depth, multiple synchronized cameras, a high-rate IMU, audio, storage, and significant compute in a single device.

That made it possible to test several questions quickly: can terrain be observed before the user reaches it, can leg motion be measured directly rather than inferred from an IMU, and can walking data be annotated without requiring the wearer to press a handheld remote?

One device, two views

My first idea attempted to route depth sensing through a mirror. Testing showed that reflected geometry produced unreliable LiDAR measurements. I therefore separated the sensing roles.

The forward-facing camera and LiDAR observe terrain directly and preserve metric depth. A second ultra-wide camera looks through a custom 45° prism mirror and is redirected downward toward the wearer's legs and feet, where RGB motion is sufficient.

Synchronized multimodal logging

I built the data logger in Swift and SwiftUI using AVFoundation and CoreMotion. The system records forward LiDAR depth at roughly 29 fps, downward ultra-wide RGB at about 30 fps, 100 Hz device motion and gravity, camera intrinsics, and audio.

Every stream is timestamped against the same host clock. This eliminated post-hoc synchronization between vision, depth, motion, and spoken activity labels, and also created a path for aligning the phone with the wearable device's own IMU streams.

Finding terrain structure in raw LiDAR depth

My first terrain-estimation methods tried to recover an absolute ground angle from the depth map and later from a fitted 3D plane. Both approaches performed poorly. The waist-mounted camera was often close to horizontal, so many frames contained walls and distant structures rather than a clean ground plane.

Instead of forcing a geometric ground model onto those frames, I inspected the raw depth maps and switched to structural depth profiles. Taking the vertical profile through the direction of travel produced physically different signatures for each terrain: flat ground changed gradually, ascending stairs formed stepped plateaus, and descending stairs occupied a narrower depth range.

A simple top-versus-bottom profile feature reached an effect size of 4.11 between flat ground and stair ascent in one session. More importantly, this analysis showed that useful terrain structure was already present in the raw consumer LiDAR data, without first reconstructing a full 3D environment.

Predicting when the wearer will reach the stair

For an assistive controller, recognizing a stair is only part of the problem. The system also needs enough lead time to prepare the assistance before the user's foot reaches it.

I low-pass filtered the forward distance trajectory to remove gait-induced oscillation, estimated closing velocity using linear regression over a recent time window, and computed time-to-contact from current distance and approach speed.

The useful prediction window was approximately 3–5 seconds before arrival. Prediction degraded closer to the stair because the wearer naturally slowed down, violating the constant-velocity assumption behind the simple TTC model. Rather than treating that as random noise, I identified deceleration itself as a repeatable behavior that a future hybrid estimator could explicitly use.

Extracting gait directly from the downward camera

For the mirror view, I used dense optical flow between consecutive frames. Because the camera moves with the torso, I subtracted the median frame-wide motion from the optical-flow magnitude to suppress common body motion and retain movement that differed locally around the legs.

The resulting one-dimensional motion signal produced a clear gait rhythm. On flat-ground walking, the extracted cadence was about 102 steps/min with a spectral SNR of 19.6.

Hands-free annotation

I also replaced manual button-based annotation with spoken terrain labels. Audio is recorded on the same time base as depth and IMU, then transcribed offline with Whisper and converted into timestamped activity intervals.

Moving speech recognition offline avoided capture lag and preserved the original audio for reprocessing. During development, I also diagnosed and corrected a repetition failure in Whisper by disabling previous-text conditioning and restructuring the keyword-matching logic.

What the prototype did — and did not — establish

H-Vision demonstrated that terrain structure, gait rhythm, and useful approach timing can all be extracted from a compact wearable consumer platform. It did not establish general-purpose terrain recognition. Performance varied between sessions and camera angles, and the strongest TTC result came from a limited number of long, straight stair approaches.

I therefore treated the phone as a reference platform rather than a finished sensor. Its role was to determine which physical signals were worth pursuing in cheaper dedicated hardware and how those signals could complement an exosuit's existing IMUs.
