Led an interdisciplinary mechanical and electrical engineering team to develop an active robotic armrest for ENT microsurgery, combining precision mechanical design, sensor-based actuation, human factors, EMG analysis, and computer-vision posture tracking.

Clinical collaboration and design requirements

The project was developed in collaboration with ENT surgeons and ergonomics experts at the Icahn School of Medicine at Mount Sinai and Mount Sinai Hospital. Through clinician feedback, OR workflow observation, and ergonomic guidance, we translated surgical needs into engineering requirements for sterility, adjustability, full forearm support, minimal footprint, and intuitive operation.

A key problem with existing passive supports is that they are difficult to reposition during a procedure once the sterile field is established. Our goal was therefore to create an active support that could be adjusted quickly without interrupting the surgical workflow.

System architecture and mechanical design

I led the system-level development and mechanical design of a stool-mounted two-link arm that provides passive horizontal motion while a self-locking lead-screw stage provides active vertical adjustment.

A NEMA 23 stepper motor and DM542T driver were selected to support up to 7 kg without back-driving. The articulated arm used 6061 aluminum, bronze bushings, and steel shafts to achieve low-friction motion, repeatable positioning, and a compact operating room footprint.

Structural sizing combined FEA with a parametric failure-mode study over load, reach, material, cross-section, and joint dimensions. The final aluminum design maintained a reported factor of safety above 7 under the project design load.

Sensor-based actuation and embedded control

To make height adjustment intuitive during surgical work, we built an embedded control system using Arduino, a ToF sensor, tactile clicker switches, and stepper-motor control.

The ToF sensor detected forearm position near the support surface, while the local controls allowed precise commanded motion without manually repositioning the mechanism.

Human-subject validation and data processing

We evaluated the prototype from three complementary perspectives: muscular effort, posture, and fine-motor precision. Preliminary testing compared supported and unsupported conditions using surface EMG, video pose estimation, and a line-tracing task.

EMG was sampled at 1 kHz and processed using RMS amplitude and median power frequency over sliding windows. For posture, side-view video was processed frame-by-frame using DWPose to extract ergonomic neck metrics.

Drawing precision was quantified by fitting each scanned stroke to a straight line and measuring RMS residual error, giving a simple quantitative measure of hand steadiness.

In the preliminary evaluation, arm support was associated with roughly 30% lower trapezius EMG amplitude, 10% lower forward-head offset, and 23% lower drawing deviation.

These results were treated as pilot validation rather than clinical efficacy claims, but they provided quantitative evidence that the mechatronic support concept could improve ergonomics without degrading fine-motor performance.
