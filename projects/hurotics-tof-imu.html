A self-initiated wearable sensing platform that combines forward-tilted Time-of-Flight sensors with bilateral thigh IMUs to recognize terrain and gait, while exploring whether unlabeled exosuit data could be annotated automatically.

Why add ranging to an IMU-based wearable?

HUROTICS' walking-assist systems use inertial sensing to estimate gait, but IMUs fundamentally describe motion that has already occurred. They do not directly observe the terrain ahead of the wearer. At the same time, large amounts of gait data are difficult to use for learning when the activity being performed is not annotated.

I explored whether inexpensive ranging sensors mounted directly on the thighs could add terrain information while also producing sensor patterns distinct enough to automatically separate gait activities without manually supplied labels.

Designing the sensor geometry

A vertically mounted distance sensor mostly measures the distance directly below the leg. On a slope, the wearer remains approximately aligned with gravity, so this measurement changes very little. I therefore tilted each ToF sensor roughly 10° forward so that its beam intersects terrain ahead of the wearer.

The geometry can be approximated by L = H / (cos θ + sin θ · tan α), where L is measured distance, H is sensor height, θ is beam angle from vertical, and α is terrain slope. When θ = 0, terrain slope disappears from the equation, explaining why the forward tilt is important.

From prototype to standalone wearable logger

The final system used two VL53L4CD ToF sensors and two BNO085 IMUs, one pair per thigh, with a Teensy 4.1 handling sensor acquisition, activity buttons, status indication, and SD-card logging.

Several iterations were required before the system became reliable. Both ToF sensors initially shared the same I²C address, so I used their shutdown pins to bring them online sequentially and reassign unique addresses. More significantly, the BNO085 repeatedly failed over I²C during development. After isolating the problem from wiring and bus assignment, I moved the IMUs to UART-RVC mode and migrated the logger to a Teensy 4.1 with dedicated serial ports.

Running all four sensors exposed another concurrency problem: blocking ToF reads allowed incoming IMU serial packets to overflow the receive buffers. I enlarged the buffers and implemented a drain-to-latest strategy so each logged ToF sample was paired with the newest available IMU measurement.

Did the forward tilt actually help?

Yes. During stair descent, the forward-tilted configuration increased the measured distance by approximately 221 mm, compared with about 75 mm for the vertical mount — roughly a threefold increase in terrain signal.

Can the activities separate without human labels?

I segmented the bilateral ToF and thigh-pitch signals into time windows and extracted statistical features from left distance, right distance, left-right difference, and both thigh angles. Using leakage-free time-block cross-validation, ToF + IMU classification reached 99.0% accuracy on stair activities.

The more interesting question was whether the activity labels were needed at all. I removed them during training and applied K-means clustering to the sensor features. After clustering, the resulting groups matched the human button labels with 97.7% agreement and an Adjusted Rand Index of 0.93.

This changed the way I viewed the project. Rather than only acting as another terrain classifier, the wearable could potentially serve as a low-cost annotation front end for large existing IMU datasets — automatically organizing field data before it is used to train more advanced assistive-control models.

What did not generalize

Stair activities were strongly separated, but ramps were much harder. Unsupervised agreement dropped to about 68%, and outdoor sunlight substantially increased ToF measurement uncertainty. The dataset also contained only one participant across seven sessions, so these results should be interpreted as a proof of concept rather than cross-subject validation.

The work reinforced an important lesson: a high classification number is useful only after checking the data split, session dependence, and physical sensing conditions. Several initially high scores dropped when I removed leakage from overlapping time windows, while the stair result remained strong.
