Mechanical design and validation hardware for wearable exosuits, spanning an anthropomorphic endurance test jig, tendon-routing components, user-facing attachment hardware, tool-free docking, and thermal validation.

Anthropomorphic endurance test jig

HUROTICS needed a robotic test fixture that could wear a complete exosuit and reproduce gait motion continuously for product validation. I translated system-level requirements into the mechanical architecture, actuator sizing, packaging constraints, and control concept for the test rig.

The system was designed around 200 N cable loading, 100,000 gait cycles, and walking speeds from 0.2 to 6 km/h. I developed a torque budget that included cable reaction force, leg weight, and acceleration, resulting in a peak requirement of approximately 29 to 32 N·m.

An earlier motor, gearbox, and encoder architecture exceeded the available hip-width envelope. I replaced it with a compact integrated actuator and performed packaging and interference checks to keep both hip drives within human-scale geometry while preserving torque and speed margin.

The concept was approved for external fabrication, and HUROTICS committed approximately $15,000 to build the production test system based on the proposed architecture.

Tendon routing sheath

For the next-generation daily-use exosuit, I designed the sheath that redirects the tendon load around the hip. The component acts as a critical mechanical interface between the actuator cable and the body, so cable path, bending radius, friction, and joint kinematics directly affect force transmission.

I adjusted the sheath entry and exit geometry around the changing joint configuration to reduce sharp cable curvature, parasitic friction, and interference across the required range of motion.

Self-locking thigh attachment

I designed the hook connecting the tendon cable to the thigh garment as both a load-bearing component and a human interface. The geometry needed to withstand repeated tensile loading while remaining easy to locate, grip, and attach for patients and older users.

I separated the garment-side component from the cable assembly to simplify production and final assembly. A cross-pin installed in the cable end allows the hook to capture the cable mechanically, while the load path becomes more secure under tension. I used FEA and iterative FDM prototypes to refine the structure, grip geometry, and attachment orientation.

Tool-free drive-unit docking

The motor and battery module needed to be removable from the back-mounted wearable assembly without tools. I designed a guided linear docking interface with snap-fit retention so the module could follow a constrained insertion path and positively lock into place.

The design required careful fit and clearance control because the sliding and locking features were produced with FDM prototypes. I incorporated approximately 0.15 to 0.30 mm sliding clearance and iterated the snap-fit geometry to balance insertion force, retention, repeatability, and manufacturability.

Drive-unit thermal validation

The wearable drive unit contains the motor electronics and battery in a compact enclosure, making thermal management another packaging constraint. I designed and fabricated a full-scale test enclosure around the actual cooling hardware to reproduce the expected operating environment.

The test bed incorporated one intake fan, two exhaust fans, ventilation openings, internal component packaging, and removable vent geometry. This allowed the cooling architecture to be tested under representative enclosure conditions before committing to the final product housing.
