Mechanical design and validation hardware for wearable exosuits, spanning an anthropomorphic endurance test jig, tendon-routing components, tool-free docking hardware, and a thermal test platform.

Anthropomorphic endurance test jig

HUROTICS needed a robotic test fixture that could wear a complete exosuit and reproduce gait motion continuously for long-duration product validation. I developed the mechanical architecture, actuator sizing, and control concept around a real mannequin torso and custom lower-body mechanism.

The design targeted 200 N cable loading, 100,000 gait cycles, and walking speeds from 0.2 to 6 km/h. I built a torque budget including cable reaction force, leg weight, and acceleration, giving a peak requirement of roughly 29 to 32 N·m. This led to selection of a compact integrated actuator that could fit within human hip-width constraints while maintaining torque and speed margin.

The concept was approved for external fabrication, with HUROTICS committing approximately $15,000 to build the production test system based on the proposed architecture.

Tendon routing sheath

For the next-generation daily-use exosuit, I designed the sheath that redirects the tendon force around the body. Because cable routing directly affects assistive force transmission, I shaped the entry and exit geometry around hip flexion and extension to reduce sharp bends, friction, and interference throughout the joint range of motion.

Self-locking thigh hook

I also designed the interface connecting the tendon cable to the thigh garment. The hook needed to be easy for patients and older users to attach, while remaining secure under repeated tensile load.

To simplify production and assembly, I separated the garment-side interface from the cable assembly and designed the hook to capture a cross-pin installed in the cable end. The geometry becomes more secure as tensile load increases, while the outer shape was iterated for grip and handling. I used FEA to evaluate the primary load path and refine the structure before prototyping.

Tool-free drive-unit docking

The motor and battery module had to be removable from the back-mounted wearable assembly without tools. I designed a guided sliding interface with snap-fit retention so the drive unit could be inserted along a constrained path and positively locked into place.

The design incorporated manufacturing clearance for FDM prototypes, including approximately 0.15 to 0.30 mm sliding clearance, while balancing fit, retention force, accessibility, and repeatable assembly.

Drive-unit thermal test bed

To evaluate heat buildup inside the wearable drive enclosure, I designed and fabricated a full-scale test housing around the actual cooling hardware. The enclosure reproduced the drive-unit packaging with one intake fan, two exhaust fans, ventilation openings, and representative internal components.

The platform allowed the team to run the cooling system in realistic packaging conditions and evaluate temperature rise before committing to the final enclosure design.
