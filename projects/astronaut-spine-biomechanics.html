Contributed to NASA-funded astronaut biomechanics research at Wake Forest University School of Medicine, using pre/post-flight MRI data and subject-specific finite-element modeling to study how long-duration spaceflight affects spinal musculature and injury risk.

MRI-driven human body model personalization

I analyzed pre- and post-flight MRI data from nine International Space Station astronauts, measuring vertebral geometry and segmenting 19 bilateral cervical, thoracic, and lumbar muscles.

Muscle cross-sectional areas were compared with the GHBMC M50 human body model and converted into regional scaling ratios for physiological cross-sectional area, providing individualized muscle inputs for finite-element models.

Computational modeling and preprocessing

I developed Python and MATLAB tools to extract vertebral centroid coordinates, calculate cortical thickness, process landmark data, and generate LS-PrePost-compatible input files for subject-specific model development.

The workflow supported personalized LS-DYNA human body simulations used to evaluate spinal loading under multiple spacecraft landing directions, severities, and pulse conditions.

Research output

The work was supported by the NASA Human Research Program and contributed to ongoing efforts to improve astronaut-specific biomechanical models for post-flight injury assessment.

I presented first-author work on MRI-derived spinal muscle personalization at the 2025 BMES Annual Meeting.
