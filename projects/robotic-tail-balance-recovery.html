A modular inertial tail developed for quadruped balance recovery, combining cable-driven actuation, IMU feedback, active control, and dynamic experiments to study how tail segmentation affects robot stabilization.

Robotic tail design and actuation

I worked in a three-person team to develop a lightweight robotic appendage that could generate reaction torque without modifying the quadruped's legs or gait controller. The tail uses modular 3D-printed links connected by revolute joints and antagonistic cables to convert compact servo motion into coordinated planar tail bending.

The system was designed around interchangeable 1-link, 2-link, and 3-link configurations. Cable routing produced approximately 30°, 60°, and 90° of tail-tip motion respectively, allowing us to investigate how additional segmentation changes the inertial response generated from the same actuator architecture.

Quadruped integration and feedback control

We integrated the tail onto a Mini Pupper 2 quadruped using a custom mounting interface. An Arduino Nano 33 IoT provided onboard IMU sensing and servo commands, allowing the tail to react to measured body motion while the robot traversed flat, inclined, and bumped terrain.

The first active-tail experiment revealed a negative result: body roll RMS increased by 19% relative to the matched passive condition. Hardware debugging later identified a likely IMU-axis mapping error in the threshold-based controller, and the quadruped suffered a servo failure before the full active-control test matrix could be completed.

Controlled dynamics testbed

To separate tail dynamics from gait coupling, foot-ground contact, mechanical asymmetry, and quadruped controller behavior, we moved the same robotic tail onto a planar pendulum testbed. This turned the problem into a controlled disturbance-rejection experiment where the contribution of the active appendage could be measured directly.

The pendulum controller used IMU feedback with PD control to command the tail against body motion. We tested three tail configurations, three initial disturbance angles, and both passive and active control for 18 total trials.

Dynamics modeling and system identification

We modeled the body-tail system as a coupled pendulum and cable-driven inertial appendage. A Lagrangian formulation linked tail reaction torque to body motion, while the measured body response was approximated as a second-order damped system.

I used video-based motion tracking to recover body angle over time and fit an exponential envelope to oscillation peaks. This provided the decay rate and damping ratio needed to compare controller performance independently of the different pendulum configurations.

Active stabilization and segmentation

The closed-loop robotic tail increased damping in all nine matched conditions, reaching 1.26× to 1.66× the passive damping ratio. The three-segment tail produced the largest active-control gain across every tested release angle.

The main limitation is that segmentation and tail swing range were coupled by the cable transmission. The larger 3-link response therefore reflects both increased segmentation and greater available tail excursion. A future design would independently control these variables and transfer the validated PD controller back onto the quadruped.
